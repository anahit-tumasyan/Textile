Reading the recipe of a fabric

A working prototype that determines what a textile is made of — the missing piece that lets clothing waste actually be recycled.

1The problem, in one paragraph

The world throws away about 92 million tonnes of textiles every year, and less than 1% becomes new clothing. The reason is not lack of will — it is that recyclers cannot tell what a garment is made of. Care labels are missing, faded, or simply wrong, and cotton must go to a completely different recycling process than polyester. A 60/40 blend needs different treatment again.

Whoever solves the identification problem unlocks the rest.

2What we built

Shine infrared light on a fabric. Read back the recipe.

Every material absorbs infrared light in its own pattern — a fingerprint. Cotton and polyester have clearly different fingerprints. When a fabric is a blend, its fingerprint is a mixture of the two.

Our software reads that mixed fingerprint and works out the exact percentage of each fibre — for example "62% cotton, 38% polyester". Not just which fibre, but how much. That "how much" is the part nobody has solved well, and it is what makes the difference between a garment being recyclable or landfilled.

5Scope — what this prototype does and does not prove

Simulated fabric, by design

The fabrics here are simulated rather than physically measured — a deliberate first step, not a limitation discovered along the way. The infrared fingerprints are generated mathematically from the genuine published absorption bands of cotton and polyester in the scientific literature.

Why this is the right order. Real labelled data — fabric with laboratory-verified composition — is scarce and expensive to collect, and that scarcity is precisely the bottleneck this research exists to break. Simulating before measuring is standard practice in sensing research: it validates the method before committing months to sample collection.

What this means for the numbers. The accuracy figures above will change on real fabric. What the prototype establishes is that the full pipeline works end to end, and it sets the measured bar that the novel method must beat. Stating the scope plainly is what allows the results to be taken at face value.

6What happens next

This prototype is the starting step of a staged plan, and it is deliberately scoped as such.

  1. Baseline — done, this prototype Prove the method works and measure exactly how hard the problem is.
  2. Real data — what funding buys first Collect real fabrics with verified composition, including blends and dark colours. This dataset becomes our most defensible asset.
  3. The novel method — the actual research Solve a specific open problem: accurate percentages on blends, on black fabric, or using a cheap sensor instead of expensive laboratory equipment.
  4. Laboratory validation (TRL 4) Connect a real infrared sensor to the software and prove it on real samples in controlled conditions.
  5. Real waste stream (TRL 5–6) Test at an actual recycling facility with a partner, on messy real-world material.

The prototype covers step 1. Steps 2 and 3 are what the application asks to fund.
