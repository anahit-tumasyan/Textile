What is this fabric
actually made of?

Clothing labels are often wrong, and you cannot recycle a garment until you know what is in it. This prototype shines a special light on a scrap of fabric and reads back the answer as a percentage — 62% cotton, 38% polyester — in about a second.

The problem

Mixed fabrics are the part nobody has solved

About 92 million tonnes of textile waste is thrown away every year. Less than 1% of it becomes new clothing.

The reason is simple: a recycling plant has to know what a garment is made of before it can process it, and it cannot trust the label. It has to measure.

Measuring pure cotton or pure polyester is already routine. Mixtures are not. A shirt that is 60% cotton needs a different process than one that is 80% cotton, and right now there is no cheap, fast way to tell those apart. That is the gap this prototype is aimed at.

How it works

Three steps, no laboratory

  1. Shine a light on the fabric

    Not ordinary light — a kind just beyond what our eyes can see. Every material soaks up this light in its own pattern, which is where the fingerprint comes from.

  2. Tidy up the reading

    Real scraps are creased, dusty and dyed, and all of that smudges the reading. Two standard corrections wipe the smudges off and leave the part that depends on the material itself.

  3. Work out the mix

    Software that has studied thousands of fabrics with known contents turns the tidied fingerprint into a percentage. This is the difficult step, and it is the one being measured here.

Why it is difficult

The two fingerprints overlap

If cotton and polyester soaked up light in completely different places, this would be simple arithmetic. They do not.

So the software cannot just check one spot on the line. It has to weigh up the entire shape at once. That is why getting a percentage is hard, while answering “is there any polyester at all?” is easy.

What is proven, and what is not

The fabrics were imitations, not real cloth. They were built from published laboratory measurements of real cotton and real polyester, so they behave realistically — but no physical scrap has gone through this yet. The accuracy figures will move once real material does.

Nothing here is a new invention yet. Reading fabric content from light is established work. What this shows is that the whole chain can be built and honestly tested — and it sets the score that any genuinely new idea now has to beat.

Why it is still worth backing. It runs end to end today, it puts a hard number on the target (1.65), and the next step is gathering real material rather than writing more software.
