A plain-language read of the SkillVentory TRL 4 task document against what exists today. Short version: the software side is further along than the document assumes, and the two things standing between us and TRL 4 are not software at all.

The honest position

We are at TRL 2–3, aiming at 4

Everything measured so far used simulated fabric — readings built from published laboratory measurements of real cotton and real polyester, which behave realistically, but which are not physical cloth. The working pipeline is real. The validation is not yet.

Why this matters for the application: TRL 4 means the technology has been validated in a laboratory — on real material, under controlled conditions, checked by someone independent. Claiming TRL 4 on simulated data would not survive review, and a reviewer who spots it will discount everything else in the proposal. Claiming TRL 2–3 with a credible, costed route to 4 is a strong position, and it is the true one.

The two blockers

Why more code does not get us to TRL 4

1. There is no real fabric in the project. To validate, we need physical scraps whose true composition has been confirmed by chemical testing — the standard method dissolves one fibre away and weighs what remains. That is laboratory work, priced per sample, and it is the single largest cost in reaching TRL 4.

2. Nobody outside the project has tested us. Independent validation means handing our system samples chosen by someone else and publishing whatever comes back. We can build the blind-scoring system today; we cannot supply the independence.

Everything else in the task document is either done, or is a matter of engineering time that these two unlock.

Supporting evidence

The gap we are claiming is real, and the local group documented it

Researchers at the University of Tartu tested 89 real textile samples across 26 types using infrared spectroscopy and statistical analysis. They could identify what fibres were present. On the question of how much of each, their conclusion was blunt:

“obtaining accurate quantitative composition from real samples is not possible with ATR-FT-IR” Peets, Leito, Pelt & Vahur (2017), Spectrochimica Acta Part A. doi:10.1016/j.saa.2016.09.007

They could only reach approximate figures, and only by averaging 25 separate readings per sample, because real blended fabric is not evenly mixed at small scale.

How to use this in the application — and how not to

Use it as: independent, peer-reviewed confirmation from a local group that the established technique cannot deliver quantitative blend composition. That is exactly the gap this project targets, and it is far stronger coming from someone else than from us.

Do not use it as: support for our method. It is a different technique — a different part of the infrared spectrum than ours. Citing it as evidence our approach works would be wrong, and a reviewer in this field will notice.

Take the warning seriously: their finding that real fabric is unevenly mixed is precisely the effect our simulation does not reproduce. Expect real accuracy to be worse than the current figures, and plan to average several readings per sample, as they did.

This paper also identifies a natural collaborator with real samples and instruments already in Tartu — worth naming in the proposal.

What exists today

The capability evidence

Our approach1.65 points Roughly twice as accurate

“1.65 points” means that when a fabric is truly 62% cotton, the system typically answers between 60% and 64%. The improvement over the baseline is measurable and reproducible — on simulated fabric. Reproducing it on real fabric is the funded work.

A second prototype, which reads composition from an ordinary photograph rather than a laboratory sensor, is designed and built but has not seen real fabric either. It should be presented as planned work, not as a second validated system.

The ask

What the funding buys, in order

  1. Sample collection — 150–300 real blended fabrics spanning the full range of mixtures, weave types and dye colours, with black and dark fabrics deliberately over-represented, because that is where current sensors fail worst.
  2. Reference testing — confirmed composition for every sample by standard chemical analysis. The main per-sample cost, and the foundation everything else rests on.
  3. Instrument access — a benchtop near-infrared spectrometer, or a partnership with a group that already has one.
  4. Re-run the baseline and our method on the real data — the software is already written and takes real data directly. This step is fast once samples exist.
  5. Independent blind validation — a third party scores us on samples we have never seen.
  6. Laboratory demonstrator — the end-to-end workflow in the task document, running on real material.

Before we build to the spec

Two things worth settling

1. The task document measures two different tasks

WP2 asks for accuracy, precision, recall and F1 — these describe sorting things into categories (“is there polyester in this?”). WP3 asks for MAE, RMSE and R² — these describe estimating a quantity (“how much polyester?”). Our project does the second. If the baselines are measured on the first, the required “improvement over baseline” compares two things that cannot be compared. Worth agreeing which before WP2 is built.

2. The TRL claim in the proposal

The objective states TRL 4 as the target, which is right. The proposal should not state it as the current position. Recommended wording: “baseline and evaluation infrastructure complete; laboratory validation is the funded work.”
